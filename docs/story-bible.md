# Moonshine — Story Bible

This is the canonical story reference for *Moonshine*. When a map, a line of dialog, or a puzzle disagrees with this document, this document wins until someone revises it. Game structure lives in [design/overview.md](design/overview.md); the first playable slice is specified in [design/01-opening.md](design/01-opening.md). Everything here serves the client's [brief](brief.md).

## The story in one paragraph

Tessel is a steep white city of stairs and canals on an island in a sheltered bay. Every year on Moonshine Night its people set basins of water on their roofs to "catch the moon" as it rises through a notch in the mountains called the Gate. Nobody remembers why. The truth is that Tessel is a ship: a floating hill of hollow stone that the city's own ancestors built to escape a drowning homeland, moored here five centuries ago by a chain as thick as a house. The festival, the bell, the skipping rhyme children chant, and the woman who counts the steps are all scraps of a forgotten maintenance manual. A month ago the Mayor replaced the old bell-ringer with a clock, and the last link in that chain of habits broke. On Moonshine Night the anchor slips. Cheryl, a retired schoolteacher who only wanted her yearly moonlit bath, watches the moon rise in the wrong place, loses her bath plug down a drain that goes much too deep, and follows it into the dark.

## Tessel

### Geography

Tessel stands on a single steep island in **the Bowl**, a round, sheltered bay ringed by sheer mainland cliffs called **the Rim**. The Bowl's only opening to the sea is a narrow mouth on its southern side guarded by a reef called **the Teeth**. On the eastern Rim there is a deep notch, as if something took a bite out of the mountains: **the Gate**. On Moonshine Night the full moon rises exactly in the Gate, as seen from Tessel's rooftops. It has done so for as long as anyone remembers.

The island is shaped like a long loaf: high in the middle, with a pointed headland at one end (**the Prow**, with its lighthouse) and a blunt, cliffy end at the other (**the Back**). The city wraps the island in layers:

- **The Crown** — the summit plaza: the Bell Tower, the Blue Dome, a sundial, the Clockmaker's shop.
- **The Upper Terraces** — houses stacked roof-on-roof just below the Crown, joined by lanes too narrow for two people with shopping. Cheryl lives here, in Cistern Court. Lamp Street runs through, past the Little School.
- **The Hundred Steps** — the great zigzag stair down the island's face, with landings, cats, and a woman who counts.
- **Fountain Square** — the middle of town: market stalls, the Shop, the café, the Mayor's office, and the Fountain of the Fish.
- **The Bilge** — the lowest quarter, where canals cut into the island's foot. Little bridges, the fish market, boats tied to doorsteps, and the Water Stairs, which go down into the water and keep going. Residents believe "the Bilge" is a joke about the smell.
- **The Harbor** — the quay, the ferry landing, and the road out along the Prow to the lighthouse.
- **The Back** — the steep rear of the island: goat paths, cliff gardens, and a cove where Captain Mott keeps a shack and a still.

A ferry crosses the Bowl to the mainland twice a week. There are no cars on Tessel and there never could be. There are about four thousand people, eleven thousand cats (by the Mayor's estimate), and one telephone, which is in the Mayor's office and has never rung.

### Feel

Whitewashed walls gone gold in the evening. Blue shutters, blue doors, blue domes. Terracotta pots of geraniums on every step. Laundry strung across lanes. Stairs everywhere — up to a door, down to a terrace, round a corner to a stair you didn't know was there. Below, green canal water slapping against painted boats. Sun on stone all day; lamps and gossip all evening. It's warm and slightly shabby, and people tend it with love.

It should feel like a real place with its own habits. It isn't Santorini or Venice. Tessel's look comes from the fact that it was built *on top of something* and never quite fits it: walls lean on older walls, some stairs end in midair, doorsteps are set with round blue glass studs that everyone calls *moon-eyes*, and floors are rarely level. The city has no grand palaces; its biggest buildings are the Bell Tower and the Blue Dome.

Time period: deliberately vague, roughly a century ago. Oil lamps and gaslight, no electricity, no radios. The Mayor's new clock is the most modern thing on the island, and he would like you to know it.

### Daily life

Tessel lives on fish, tourists (a trickle, by ferry), terrace gardens, and gossip. People shout to each other across lanes from balconies. Groceries go up to high flats in baskets on ropes. Children skip rope in the Little School's yard to a rhyme older than the school. The bell on the Crown rings at odd hours that everyone has stopped noticing (it rings at the turn of the tide, not by the clock). Each late summer there is **Moonshine Night**, and three days later the **Stair Race** up the Hundred Steps.

### Moonshine Night

On the night of the late-summer full moon, every household sets a basin, bowl, or bucket of water on its roof or windowsill to *catch the moon*: to see the rising moon reflected in it the moment it clears the Gate. Then you make a wish, or drink a toast, or tip the water down the Hundred Steps in the morning for luck, depending on your family. Most people do it with a teacup. Cheryl does it with a bathtub, and gets in.

On Moonshine Night all of Tessel is up on its roofs, and there's an old, unspoken rule: **nobody looks at anybody else's roof.** Everyone faces the Gate. Cheryl's tub sits in plain view of Her Next Door, and both of them pretend otherwise, as they have for thirty-one years. Cheryl also bathes in a navy-and-cream striped bathing costume older than the Mayor, so the rule has never been tested. The lack of privacy is deliberate: it's a small comedy of good manners, and it says something about Cheryl, who has never once cared who saw her do something she loves. (Client feedback after playing the opening: the rooftop tub looked strangely public. This makes the publicness intentional and visible in play.)

The word *moonshine* runs through the game:

1. **Moonshine Night**, the festival.
2. The first word of the **skipping rhyme** ("Moonshine, moonshine, catch it in a cup…").
3. **Moonshine**, the clear and lethal spirit Captain Mott distills in his shack.
4. *Moonshine* meaning nonsense, as in "That's moonshine, Mott," which is what everyone says when he insists the island moves.
5. The Founders' word carved over the Sighting Basin, which Ottilie translates, correctly for once, as "where the moon shines in," and then misreads as proof that the Founders were bootleggers.

### The sky over Tessel

Everything about the sky must be physically right; the only thing wrong in it is where Tessel is. (Client feedback after playing the opening: the first moonrise showed a sunset glow on the moon's side of the sky.) The facts every scene follows:

- **The Gate is on the eastern Rim.** A full moon rises opposite the sun, so on Moonshine Night the sun sets behind the *western* Rim, at Cheryl's back, while the moon rises in the east, in the Gate.
- **The eastern sky at that moment holds no sunset glow.** Above the Rim it's deepening blue; just above the horizon is a soft band of rose-pink (the sunset's light reflected high in the eastern sky), and along the Rim itself a blue-grey band, the Earth's shadow rising. Before the moon clears the Rim, a small silvery brightening shows where it will come up. The moon comes up large: pale peach-gold as it clears the Rim, then cream, then white as it climbs, laying a faint peach-gold path across the Bowl. Its halo and the sky around it are cool silver. Apart from the moon itself, all the warmth in an eastward dusk view comes from the rose-lit cliffs and the lit town, never from the sky. The low sun behind Cheryl lights everything that faces her: the Rim's cliffs glow rose (alpenglow), and the town's roofs and walls catch the last warm light, with the lamps coming on. The Bowl reflects the cool eastern sky.
- **Each night after full,** the moon rises later (about half an hour to an hour later in late summer) and at a different point on the horizon. Two nights past full, it rises after dark, about an hour after sunset, and is a waning gibbous: a hair flattened on one edge. That's why "Past full: allow two fingers."
- **The Gate is wide.** Over the years the late-summer full moon's rising point wanders a little within the notch (the moon's eighteen-and-a-half-year swing), so "in the Gate" is a band, not a point. Cheryl's thirty-one ticks all sit inside it until the last few creep right, past its edge.
- **Tides:** two high waters and two low waters a day, the tide turning about every six and a quarter hours, about fifty minutes later each day. The biggest tides, spring tides, come a day or two after full and new moon. So Moonshine Night's evening tide was a big one, and the climax two nights later is bigger still ("THE SEA'S BEEN SAVING IT UP").
- **Dusk views face the light correctly:** looking east at dusk, no orange; looking west, the afterglow behind the western Rim.

### Landmarks

Each landmark has an ordinary job above ground and a forgotten one below. This is a Founder habit (see "Two jobs" below), and it's how the mystery pays off.

| Landmark | Above ground | Below ground |

## The Founders

### Who they were

Five hundred and some years ago, a people lived in a flat, wet country of marshes and canals called **the Lowland**. The sea was taking it a field at a time. Their engineers were tide-workers who had spent generations building sluices, mills, and pumps that ran on the pull of the moon, and they made a decision: if the land would not stay above the sea, they would build land that floated.

Over forty years they raised a hill of hollow stone in a dry dock on the marsh: thousands of sealed cells of a light, glassy, pumice-filled concrete they called hullstone, fitted and ribbed like the inside of a hull. They put houses on its back, a mast on its crown, and machinery in its belly. When the sea came in for good, the hill lifted, and they sailed away on it.

They drifted and sailed for a generation, looking for calm water. They found the Bowl, a flooded crater with a single narrow mouth, as still as a millpond. They dropped an anchor the size of a chapel in the middle of it, and they set the mooring by the moon: the chain's length and bearing were fixed so that, from the Sighting Basin at the bottom of the hull, the late-summer full moon would rise dead center in the Gate. Every year they checked it. If the moon sat true in the Basin, the city was where it should be.

They meant to stay a season. They stayed for good, and they were the people of Tessel. **The residents are the Founders' descendants.** The "different architectural style" underground belongs to their own great-great-grandparents. Nobody knows this, not even Ottilie, who has written three monographs on the Founders and believes they were, in order: giant eels, smugglers, and (by the end of the game) bootleggers.

Cheryl is not a descendant. She came from the mainland thirty-one years ago. The outsider ends up maintaining the inheritance.

### Architecture and technology

Above ground, Tessel is rounded, whitewashed, warm, and improvised. Below ground, the Founders' work is the opposite. (The visual rules are in [art direction](art-direction.md#the-underground-direction-for-future-sheets); this is the story behind them.)

- **Straight, precise, and dark.** Long corridors of fitted slate-dark *hullstone* blocks with no visible mortar, chamfered 45° corners instead of arches, and floors of large square plates. It's built like the inside of a ship: corridors are decks, and at regular intervals heavy chamfered *ribs* cross the ceiling like a hull's frames. Steep ladder-stairs, not spirals. Hatches, not doors.
- **Hullstone**: a dark, dense-looking stone that is secretly light: where a block is broken, its inside is a fine honeycomb of sealed air cells. That's why the hill floats.
- **Bronze** fittings gone to verdigris: valves, wheels, gear trains, speaking tubes, the windlass drum, the chain.
- **Geometric inlays** repeated along the walls, in a strict pattern language: tides as stepped lines, moon phases as squares filling up, the three pumps as three upright bars of different widths. Only the Chart Room's great floor inlay is pictorial: the city drawn as a ship. The recurring emblem is a crescent over a stepped wave, joined by a chain link.
- **Moonglass**: a pale glass that drinks light and gives it back as a cool cyan glow for days (`relic_cyan` in the palette). It lights the Founders' lamps and the light-pipes, which carry daylight down from the moon-eyes on the streets. Their locks also use it: a *moon-lock* opens when bright light falls on its glass eye. The machinery's own warning lights and the Tide Wheel's housings glow amber (`relic_amber`). The underground is lit locally by these glows; the surface by the sun.
- **Tide power.** Tidal flow through gill-like channels in the hull turns the **Tide Wheel**, which drives the **Three Sisters** (the ballast pumps: Thin, Stout, and Middle) and the **Windlass**. The Founders called the whole system, in effect, "the moon's work."
- **Sighting.** The shaft, the mirror, and the Basin form a periscope aimed at the Gate.
- **Signals.** The bell and its rope, speaking tubes, and rods connect the deck above to the machinery below.

### Two jobs

The Founders lived on a ship, where space is precious, so **nothing they made does only one thing**. The mast is a bell tower. The clapper is a spare part. The fountain is a gauge and a door. The well is a telescope. The doorstep studs are skylights. The children's rhyme is the pump manual. The festival is the annual calibration.

This is a rule for everyone writing content. Every Founder object has two jobs, and the city has forgotten one of them. When a player figures out the second job, that's the puzzle solution. Plant the first job early and in plain sight.

### How it was forgotten

The Founders kept a guild of **Keepers** who lived Downstairs and ran the machinery. Above them, life got easy. About three hundred years ago a storm surge yanked the chain and the city lurched — Tessel remembers this as **the Great Shake**. The Keepers' Stair under the Crown collapsed and most of the Keepers died. The survivors sealed the dangerous ways down: the city mortared over the hundredth step, and the last Keeper bolted the Founders' Door from the inside and never came up. (Cheryl finds the Keeper's chair, cup, and ledger in the Chart Room. The last entry reads: *Rang at the turn. All well.*)

After that, "Downstairs" was something you said to frighten children, and the Keepers' duties survived only as habits:

| Habit (what Tessel thinks it is) | What it really was |
| --- | --- |
| The bell "at odd hours," rung by the Tull family from a ledger of tide times | Ringing at the turn of the tide pulls the linkage that lets the Tide Wheel wind the chain |
| Moonshine Night: catch the moon in a basin | The annual sighting that checks the mooring |
| The skipping rhyme | The order for waking the Three Sisters, the warning about the Middle one, and the windlass and sighting steps |
| The Keeper of the Count, a civic title held by a woman who counts the Hundred Steps every day | Once counted the chain links paid out at each tide |
| The Stair Race | The Keepers' messengers ran alarms up the Steps |
| "Ninety-nine steps up to the Crown, and the hundredth step goes down" | A plain description of the Keepers' hatch |

The habits wore down over time. The bell was the last one still doing its real job, until five weeks ago.

## The Wrongness

### What it is

**Tessel has slipped its mooring.** Underneath that are three specific failures:

1. **The Tooth broke.** When the Mayor's clock took over the bell, the bell stopped ringing at the turn of the tide, and the windlass stopped being wound. For five weeks the chain crept out a link at a time, and the strain built on the windlass's pawl, a bronze tooth the size of a person. On Moonshine Night the Tooth sheared (the crack and groan Cheryl hears) and the chain ran out a long way. The city swung and lurched. It happened on Moonshine Night *because* of the moon: the full moon brings the spring tides, the month's biggest, and the evening high water at moonrise was one heave more than the Tooth could take. (This is also why the Founders calibrated on the full moon: it's the moment of greatest strain.) As the hull dropped and swung, it pulled a gust of air down the Sighting Shaft, which sucked Cheryl's plug down her drain. The same air breathes back out of the court well the next morning. Ever since, Tessel has been drifting slowly across the Bowl toward the Teeth.
2. **The ballast is failing.** The Three Sisters have not pumped in decades. The Middle Sister's copper throat was swept away long ago. (It ended up in a sea cave, where Mott's grandfather found it and built a still around it.) The ballast cells have been slowly filling unevenly, and the lurch cracked one of them. Tessel is starting to list toward the Bilge side.
3. **No one sights the moon anymore.** The Sighting Basin is dry and its stopper is lost, so nothing confirms where the city is. The city has crept out of true for years; Cheryl's moon chart shows it.

### How it threatens the city

If nothing is done, the list floods the Bilge and the drift carries the city onto the Teeth, where the hull breaks open. Tessel sinks, or breaks up and goes out to sea in pieces. Nobody above ground can see this coming, because nobody knows the city can move.

### Symptoms residents have noticed

Each of these is a real effect of the Wrongness. Each resident has a wrong explanation for it, and the wrong explanations are where the jokes come from.

- The moon rose to the right of the Gate on Moonshine Night. *(Everyone: "a funny atmosphere." Neighbor: her bleach.)*
- Moonshine Night sheets came out grey. *(Neighbor: the moon "wasn't straight on." She is correct.)*
- The ferry can't find its landing on the first try; Captain Mott swears the island has moved. *(Everyone: "That's moonshine, Mott.")*
- The Clockmaker's perfect clock disagrees with the sundial by eleven minutes, and it's getting worse. (The island has swung on its chain, so the sundial no longer faces true north.) *(Clockmaker: the sun is wrong. He has written to the mainland observatory to complain.)*
- Things roll. Marbles in the Shop end up in one corner; the Old Man's chess pieces slide toward the lemon tree; a piano goes down the Squeeze on its own. *(Everyone: old floors.)*
- The well in Cistern Court breathes cold air that smells of the sea. *(Kid: "it BREATHED on me.")*
- The Fountain runs low. *(Mayor: water thieves.)*
- The Bilge's lowest street floods at high tide. *(Fishmonger: the sea is jealous of her.)*
- The Tourist's guidebook map has the lighthouse pointing the wrong way. *(Tourist: the guidebook is wrong. It's partly wrong.)*
- The cats have all moved to the uphill side of every street. *(Nobody has noticed but Cheryl.)*

### How it escalates, and how Cheryl makes it look worse

The city's real danger grows steadily. What the city *sees* grows in jumps, and most of the jumps are Cheryl's doing. The player should feel complicit right up until the end.

| When | What happens | Whose fault it looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Act 1 | The moon rises off the Gate; the city lurches; the piano escapes; Cheryl's plug is sucked down the drain. | Nobody's. It's the first glimpse. |
| Act 3 | Cheryl opens the Step Gallery's light shafts. That night, every moon-eye on every doorstep in Tessel glows blue. | Hers. The city is delighted and spooked. "Haunted," "a miracle," "the Pelletier boys. Who are on the mainland. Which is exactly what they'd want you to think." |
| Act 3 | To drain the flooded passage to the Chart Room, Cheryl turns a valve Downstairs. It diverts the Fountain's feed, and the Fountain of the Fish stops for the first time in five hundred years. The valve jams; it can't be turned back. | Hers, unavoidably. Residents believe the list started because of it. It didn't, but the timing is awful. |
| Act 4 | The Bilge floods ankle-deep; houses lean; the bell rings at "twenty past" because Cheryl has put Ambrose back on the rope. | Partly hers, in the city's eyes. |
| Act 4→5 | Cheryl wakes the Three Sisters to fix the list. Mott's still-coil throat won't let the Middle Sister stop. **The city rises six feet out of the water. Every canal in the Bilge drains dry.** The city now rides high, catches the wind, and drifts faster. Meanwhile, with the Fountain's feed jammed shut by Cheryl in Act 3, the pumps' main has nowhere to send its water: it bursts in the Pump Hall and forces water up the Fountain shaft, so the Fountain refills (and its hatch, P2, is under water for good). Her doing, twice over. | Very much hers. This is the changed-surface reveal (see Act 4). |
| Act 5 | From the lighthouse, the Teeth are visible and closer each time Cheryl looks. | Hers, she suspects. |
| Act 6 | Cheryl releases the windlass brake. The whole city lurches; everyone falls over; the piano makes its last journey. Then everything settles, the canals refill, and the moon sits in the Gate. | Nobody's, as far as the city knows. |

### How the Wrongness looks and sounds

The Wrongness isn't a creature; it's the city being *out of true*. The [art](art-direction.md#the-wrongness) and [audio](audio-direction.md#the-three-worlds) directions already express that: misregistered pixels, colors that slip, notes that sag flat. Use those treatments only where the player is *feeling* the city come loose: the moonrise, the groan and the crack, the lurches, the Bilge flooding, the reveal. Never use them for a monster, a villain, or a glowing evil thing. There isn't one. Scale the audio's global Wrongness amount with the escalation table above: about 0.1 after Act 1, 0.2 in Act 3, 0.35 in Act 4, 0.5 in Act 5 while the reef approaches, and 0 the moment the moon centers in the Basin.

## Cheryl

### Who she is

Cheryl is fifty-eight. She came to Tessel on the ferry thirty-one years ago with one suitcase and someone else's, and stayed. She taught at the Little School on Lamp Street for twenty-nine years, so she taught half the town to read, including the Mayor, the Clockmaker, the Fishmonger, the Lighthouse Keeper, and both Piano Movers. She retired two years ago, early and by choice ("Twenty-nine years. I wanted to see what the town does in the afternoons."). She lives alone at No. 9 Cistern Court, a tall narrow house at the top of town. She bought it for the roof.

On her roof sits a cast-iron clawfoot bathtub. It took four men and a song to get it up there. Every Moonshine Night she fills it with cold water, gets in, and watches the moon rise in the Gate. She has not missed one in thirty-one years. She keeps a **moon chart** of every one.

### The gaps (never fill these in)

The game shows these and never explains them. Writers must not resolve them, hint at an answer, or let another character explain them. They are the player's to fill.

- The moon chart's first nineteen years are ticked in two hands. Cheryl only says, "The neat ticks are mine."
- There are two chairs on her roof. "I only sit in the one."
- There is a photograph on her side table, face down. "I know what it is."
- "It took four men and a song to get it up here. The song was the hard part."
- She used to play the piano. She stopped. ("Before.")
- Whose was the second suitcase?

### Personality

- **Observant.** Thirty years watching a classroom. She notices who is lying, who is hungry, and which way the cats are sitting.
- **Dry.** Her humor is understatement. She never jokes at anyone's expense who can't take it, and she's hardest on herself and on pompous people.
- **Ritual-loving and stubborn.** She does things the way she does them. A cold bath on Moonshine Night. The plug lives on the hook by the door.
- **Quietly kind.** She fixes things without telling anyone. This is exactly the person who can save a city and never mention it.
- **Nosy, professionally.** She was a teacher. Curiosity isn't a vice in her; it's a habit she hasn't broken.
- **Brave in a practical way, not a dramatic one.** "I'm brave, not stupid."
- **Vigorous.** She's a fit, capable woman in her late fifties who has climbed Tessel's stairs every day for thirty-one years. She climbs ladders and ropes, hauls on levers, and walks the whole island without remark. Her age shows in her wit and her memory, never in her body: no aches, no frailty, no "at my age." When she declines something, the reason is judgment, manners, or comedy, not weakness. (Client feedback after playing the opening: Cheryl read older than 58. This rule fixes that.)
- **Blind spot:** she believes she's an outsider who watches Tessel. By the end, she is the person holding it together.

### What she wants

**At the start:** her Moonshine Night bath, the same as every year.

**After Act 1:** to know why the moon came up in the wrong place. *Also, she would like her plug back.* The comedy of the second motive should never quite go away; the plug is a small goal that runs under the big one, and it pays off.

**At the end:** she gets the best bath of her life, though not the one she planned. Her bath plug went down the drain on Moonshine Night and fell all the way to the bottom of the Sighting Shaft. When Cheryl reaches the Sighting Basin after the climax, she finds it lying in the dry stone bowl. The Basin's own stopper was lost centuries ago. Her plug fits. She plugs the Basin, it fills, and the moon's reflection appears in it. Once she trims the mooring true, the moon sits dead center in the Gate. Then she takes off her shoes and climbs into the city's oldest bath, at the bottom of the hull, with the moon in the water around her.

### Voice

Cheryl never speaks in dialog. NPCs talk *at* her, and her silence is often the joke ("Don't look at me like that. You've looked at me like that since I was seven."). She speaks to the player in two ways:

**Descriptions** (when she examines something): first person, present tense, short. Each text box makes one observation and adds one turn. She uses specific nouns and avoids adjectives where a noun will do. She knows everyone and what they were like at seven.

> The lemon tree. It produces three lemons a year and expects applause for each one.

> The Mayor. I taught him long division. Twice.

> Someone has tried to bring a piano up the Squeeze. The Squeeze has won.

**Internal monologue** (only at significant moments: the opening, the first glimpse, the first time Downstairs, the Chart Room reveal, the surface reveal, the climax, and the ending): set in italics, quieter, sometimes only a word. The humor drops away. Budget: **two or three moments per act**, each a few boxes, at the act's turning points. Everything else, including realizations and reactions, is a plain description. If a line works as a description, it isn't monologue. (Revised from "a dozen in the whole game" after review: a dozen was too few for six acts, but the review was right that monologue was creeping into routine beats.)

> *No.*
> *It comes up in the Gate. It has come up in the Gate every year of my life.*

> *Something's wrong. Not wrong like a dropped stitch. Wrong like a step that isn't there.*

Rules for her voice:
- Never explains her own past (see the gaps above).
- Never says "I can't do that." Give each refusal a reason in character: "I'm not climbing over a piano I didn't order." "I'm brave, not stupid."
- Never makes her sound old or frail (see "Vigorous" above).
- When something is genuinely awe-inspiring, she's brief and plain. Downstairs she gets quieter.
- She calls people by her own nicknames in descriptions ("Her Next Door," "the Mayor, who was Duncan when he was seven"). Their in-game labels stay descriptive.

### Look

[Art direction](art-direction.md#cheryl) owns her design: silver hair in a bun with a coral tie, a coral cardigan over a cream blouse, a navy skirt, sensible brown shoes, and a leather satchel across her body, where the inventory lives. She walks briskly and stops to look at things. Text should agree with the sprite (her "bag" is the satchel).

## Cast

Most people are known by description. Three people besides Cheryl have names, because they carry the story. Every character has a reason to exist, a comic engine (an obsession, neurosis, foible, or blind spot), and a job in a puzzle or the plot.

### Named characters

**Ambrose Tull, the Bell-ringer** (Crown). Seventy-eight. He rang the Crown bell for fifty-two years from his grandmother's ledger of tide times, as Tulls have for three centuries, until five weeks ago, when the Mayor installed the clock and retired him "with a plaque." He is mostly deaf from the bell and speaks ENTIRELY IN CAPITALS. He is right about everything that matters ("CLOCKS DON'T KNOW THE SEA") and impossible to be around, which is why nobody listens. He sits at the foot of the tower every day to "visit the bell." *Role:* his **Tull Ledger** gives the turn of the tide and, on its flyleaf, his grandmother's version of the skipping rhyme. In Act 4, Cheryl gets him back onto the bell rope, which restarts the Tide Wheel. In the climax he rings at the turn: "the bell says when." *Ending:* the clock is removed for "disagreeing with the sun," and he gets his job back. He never learns why it mattered.

**Ottilie Brask, the Archivist** (Blue Dome). Fifty-six, Cheryl's oldest friend in Tessel and partner in a two-person book club. She is the city's expert on the Founders and has never once been right about them, nor once been in doubt. She is badly claustrophobic and will never go Downstairs: she studies the underground entirely from the Archive. *Blind spot:* she disbelieves anything directly in front of her. When Cheryl walks out of the Founders' Door, which has been bolted for three hundred years, Ottilie decides Cheryl must have come in the front way and "gone round behind." *Role:* Act 2, the folk saying about the hundredth step, plus paper and charcoal for rubbings. Act 3, the Founders' Door, which becomes a two-way passage once Cheryl unbolts it. Act 4, she reads the glyphs on Cheryl's rubbing of the ship plan and names the three pumps "Thin, Stout, and Middle" (she thinks they are types of eel), which ties the rhyme to the machines. *Ending:* she publishes a fourth monograph proving the Founders were bootleggers.

**Captain Mott, the Ferryman** (Harbor; shack at the Back). Sixties. He runs the ferry to the mainland and distills moonshine in his shack. For a month he has insisted the island moves: the landing isn't where it was, the Gate is "a hand to the left." Everyone says "That's moonshine, Mott," and he drinks more, which doesn't help his case. *Role:* his still's copper coil is the Middle Sister's lost **copper throat** (his grandfather found it in the sea cave). He trades it in Act 4 when Cheryl shows him her moon chart, the first evidence that someone else has seen what he's seen. "Thirty-one years and it creeps right. You see it too." He's so relieved he gives up the still: "I've been drinking to forget I'm right." He also gives her his heavy square jug, which she needs in Act 5. *Ending:* the first ferry back. He looks at the Gate, nods, and says nothing to anyone. He's the only resident who half-knows, and he keeps it to himself.

## The skipping rhyme

The Founders taught their children this rhyme so that someone would always remember how to run the machinery. Over five hundred years its meaning has worn away, but most of the words have survived. This is the **true version**, as written on the flyleaf of the Tull Ledger by Ambrose's grandmother:

> *Moonshine, moonshine, catch it in a cup,*
> *ring the bell when the tide turns up.*
> *Thin sister, Stout sister, Middle sister three,*
> *wake them one by one and they'll drink up the sea.*
> *Middle sister's greedy, put her back to bed,*
> *or up jumps Tessel and bumps her head.*
> *Pull the old tooth when the bell says when,*
> *wind her in slowly, and home again.*
> *Moon in the Gate and moon in the bowl,*
> *Tessel's asleep and Tessel is whole.*
> *(JUMP OUT!)*

What each line means:
- Lines 1–2: the sighting; ring at the turn of the tide to engage the Tide Wheel.
- Lines 3–4: wake the pumps in order: Thin, then Stout, then Middle.
- Lines 5–6: the Middle Sister must be stopped once the list is corrected, or the city rises. *(In the Pump Hall Cheryl can't stop her, because Mott's still coil sticks open. This foreshadows the reveal. She finally puts her to bed in Act 6, with the Keepers' master stop in the Sighting Room.)*
- Lines 7–8: replace the broken Tooth; release the windlass brake on the bell; let the tide wind the chain home.
- Lines 9–10: plug the Basin, sight the moon, trim the mooring true.

The Skipping Girls' versions (each wrong in one place that matters):
- **First Girl:** "*Stout sister, Thin sister, Middle sister three…*" (wrong order) "…*Middle sister's ready, put her in the shed.*"
- **Second Girl:** "*Thin sister, Stout sister, Little sister three…*" (right order; "Little" is wrong). She skips the greedy-sister couplet entirely: "That's the bit where you jump out."
- **Third Girl:** "*Thin sister, Fat sister, Middle sister three…*" (right order; "Fat" means Stout) "…*or up jumps Tessel and bumps her head!*" (She thinks this is the funniest line in the world.)

## The plot

The game runs from Moonshine Night through the next few days. Time of day changes only at scripted moments (dusk, day, dusk again, night); there is no clock and no deadline. The player is never on a timer. (The Kid's relay run in Act 5 looks like a race against time, but it's a scripted scene.)

### Act 1 — Moonshine Night (Cistern Court)

*Confined. Teaches the controls and the puzzle style. About 8–12 minutes.*

At sunset, Cheryl is on her roof beside an empty tub. Three things stand between her and her bath: the Old Man borrowed her plug as a chess king; Her Next Door's sheets hang right across the view of the Gate; and the only way out of the court, the narrow stair called the Squeeze, is blocked by a piano someone has tried to deliver to No. 9 (she didn't order a piano). She can't leave, but she doesn't want to. She swaps her pepper mill for the plug, trades her egg timer to the Sweaty Kid for the clothes prop so Her Next Door can move the sheets, and fills the tub.

Just as she settles in to watch, the moon rises to the right of the Gate. The crowd noise from the city stops. Something groans far below, like an enormous rope taking strain, and then cracks. The roof tilts; the sheets swing sideways; somewhere a piano goes down the Squeeze with a long falling chord. Her plug is yanked down the drain, chain and all, and the echo falls away much farther than the drain should go. (Her drain empties into the Sighting Shaft, and the lurch pulled a gust of air down it; see "What it is" above.)

The next morning, the Squeeze is clear (the piano took the geraniums with it), the well breathes cold sea air, and Tessel is open. Full design: [design/01-opening.md](design/01-opening.md).

### Act 2 — The Morning After (the wider city)

*The city opens up. About 30–40 minutes.*

Tessel is buzzing about the "crooked moon," and everyone has a wrong theory. Cheryl explores the Crown, Lamp Street, the Hundred Steps, Fountain Square, the Bilge, the Harbor, and the Back. She meets Ambrose grumbling under his silenced bell, Ottilie and her theories, Captain Mott and his stranded ferry, the Skipping Girls chanting nonsense, and the Tourist whose guidebook puts the lighthouse on the wrong end of the island. Her clues so far: the drain that went down too far, Ottilie's saying ("ninety-nine steps up to the Crown, and the hundredth step goes down"), the Counting Woman's ninety-nine, and the Kid's step that "went bonk."

The piano, meanwhile, has rolled down to the foot of the Hundred Steps and jammed in the arch to Fountain Square, so the direct route down is blocked. The long way round is the Goat Path down the Back.

Three threads lead to the first way Downstairs: get the Movers' crowbar by soaping the piano's runners so it slides off (and they chase it); get the Tourist's guidebook by a chain of trades (the Lighthouse Keeper's unwanted telescope goes into the Shop window in place of the straw hat, which goes to the Tourist); and get the Tide-Sheet from the Man on Bench, once Cheryl spots his glasses in his wife's hair. Both print "ninety-nine," and twice is enough to send the Counting Woman off her step to the Mayor. Then Cheryl watches the morning's Moonshine water, poured down the Steps for luck, vanish into the seam of one step. She pries up the hundredth step.

### Act 3 — Downstairs (first descent)

*The underground and the big middle reveal. About 25–35 minutes.*

Under the hundredth step is the **Step Gallery**, a long, straight, low deck of dark fitted hullstone, ribbed like the inside of a hull, with verdigris bronze and dead moonglass lamps. *(Internal monologue, the first time Cheryl stands in it.)* She forces a seized lever with the crowbar: the Keepers' master shutter. A light shaft opens over a bronze mirror, and a run of clunks goes away up the hill as every light-pipe under every doorstep in Tessel opens for the first time since the Great Shake. She swings the mirror until daylight falls on a moon-lock. A sealed hatch opens, and she gains a **moonglass disc** that glows and lights her way. A hidden chute drops her, one-way, into the **Lamp Halls**. She finds a ladder she can drop to make the Step Gallery a two-way shortcut, and a black flooded corridor heading downhill that she wisely refuses to swim. The passage north toward the Chart Room is flooded too, by a pipe gushing fresh water. A big valve wheel beside it diverts the flow, but its handle is gone, leaving a square spindle the crowbar slips off. In the pitch-dark Keepers' bunk room, once she relights its lamp with the disc, a bronze valve key hangs on the tool rack. When she turns the wheel with it, the passage drains, the wheel jams for good, and from a speaking tube far above comes a tiny, outraged voice: "The FOUNTAIN!"

At the north end is the **Chart Room** under the Blue Dome: the last Keeper's chair, cup, and ledger (*Rang at the turn. All well.*), and a floor mosaic, lit from above through the Dome's glass eye, showing **Tessel as a ship**: the hull, the mast where the Bell Tower stands, the chain from the Prow to an anchor, the Three Sisters, the Basin under Cistern Court. *(Internal monologue: "Oh." … "Oh, we're a boat.")* She takes a rubbing, the **Ship Plan**, which becomes the player's underground map. Then she unbolts the Founders' Door and walks out into the Archive crypt, to Ottilie's indignation.

She surfaces at dusk to find every moon-eye in Tessel glowing blue (her shafts did that) and the Fountain silent (her valve did that).

### Act 4 — The List

*Three parallel threads, ending in the changed-surface reveal. About 35–50 minutes.*

The city is listing. The Bilge floods ankle-deep at high tide, houses lean, the piano creeps again, and the town blames the Fountain going dry, which Cheryl knows is her doing. From the Ship Plan she understands the problem: the ballast pumps, the Three Sisters, are dead, and the Tide Wheel that powers them stopped when the bell stopped. Three threads, in any order:

- **The bell.** Get the Clockmaker out of the belfry (his noon pilgrimage to the sundial), lift the clock's drive off the old bell linkage, and let Ambrose back to his rope. He rings at the turn, "AT TWENTY PAST, BECAUSE THAT'S WHEN THE SEA SAYS," and far below, the Tide Wheel starts turning. The Mayor, interrupted mid-speech in front of the cordoned-off Fountain, storms off to the Bell Tower, which clears the way to the Fountain hatch.
- **The copper throat.** Show Captain Mott the moon chart. He gives up his still's coil, the Middle Sister's lost throat, and his square jug.
- **The order.** Collect the three Skipping Girls' versions of the rhyme, Ambrose's flyleaf, and Ottilie's reading of the rubbing's glyphs. Together they give the order: Thin, Stout, Middle. And a warning nobody has quite explained: *put her back to bed, or up jumps Tessel.*

Down through the drained Fountain into the **Pump Hall**: Cheryl fits the copper throat, wakes the Sisters in order, and they cough to life one after another, like old women at a funeral. The floor levels; the list is fixed. Then she tries to put the Middle Sister back to bed, and she won't stop. The still coil has spent forty years making moonshine and has forgotten how. The deck keeps rising. Water roars into the passage behind her. Cheryl climbs a long emergency ladder (one-way), up and up while the roar fades into an odd silence and then gull cries that are much too close, and shoulders open a hatch in the Fishmonger's ice cellar.

**The reveal.** She climbs the cellar stairs into daylight. The camera pans slowly along the Bilge: **every canal is empty.** Boats lie on their sides in green mud. Crabs pick across the canal beds. The Boatman sits upright in his boat on dry ground. The Fishmonger screams. The piano sits at the bottom of the main canal. The Water Stairs go down ten more steps than they ever have, to a huge bronze door crusted with barnacles, set in canal walls that turn out, below the old waterline, to be Founder slate. The town theme plays, thin and strange. *(Internal monologue: "…Ah.")*

The city has risen six feet out of the sea. Tessel thinks the sea has gone out. "The sea doesn't go OUT, Brenda, it's the SEA."

### Act 5 — High and Dry

*The danger is visible now. About 30–45 minutes.*

Riding high, Tessel catches the wind and drifts faster. From the lighthouse gallery, the Teeth are plainly visible, and closer each time Cheryl looks. The Lighthouse Keeper finally looks up from her lamp: "There's a REEF. Why is there a REEF? Twenty-two years and nobody mentions a REEF?" The Mayor announces Dry Canal Day. The Ship Plan shows what's left to do: replace the broken Tooth at the windlass, then sight the moon at the Basin.

Two threads:

- **The Water Stairs door.** Get the Boatman out of the way (a bucket from the Fishmonger, filled at the refilled Fountain: "AFLOAT!") and take his rope for the broken last flight. The barnacled moon-lock needs a full charge of light. The Lighthouse Keeper has turned round at last and wants her telescope back to watch the reef; the Shopkeeper swaps it back for the guidebook, and in return the Keeper lets Cheryl hold the moonglass disc in the great lamp. But the charge fades in less than a minute, and the door is the length of the town away: nobody could walk it in time. Someone would have to run flat out and jump off the quay. She hands the disc to the Sweaty Kid, the fastest thing in Tessel, who still has her egg timer from Moonshine Night and winds it himself. *Run.* He beats the egg. (A scripted scene; it can't fail.)
- **The Tooth.** The Clockmaker's doorstop, the bell's old clapper, is the spare Tooth. He trades it for Mott's square jug: "Square. Heavy. Doesn't roll. Smells of… is that turpentine?"

The rope and the door are separate threads (the Kid jumps down for the relay; Cheryl needs the rope for herself), so Act 5 has three threads in all. Full design: [design/05-high-and-dry.md](design/05-high-and-dry.md).

The bow passage, the Windlass: links taller than Cheryl, a bronze drum like a chapel on its side, the sheared Tooth. She levers out the stump with the crowbar and seats the clapper in its socket. It fits as if it was waiting. Ambrose has said the next turn of the tide is at moonrise. She sits on a coil of chain to wait. **Moonrise.**

### Act 6 — Moon in the Bowl (climax and ending)

*About 15–25 minutes, with the epilogue walk. Full design: [design/06-moon-in-the-bowl.md](design/06-moon-in-the-bowl.md).*

Night. Far above, the bell rings at the turn. *Pull the old tooth when the bell says when.* She throws the brake. The Tide Wheel bites, the drum turns, and the chain comes home link by link, each one a thunderclap. Cut to the surface: the whole city lurches; the Dry Canal Day crowd falls over; Her Next Door's sheets stand straight out; the piano rolls along the canal bed into the Boatman's boat; through the lighthouse window the Teeth slide away into the dark.

Cheryl walks the **Long Keel**, a straight corridor down the center of the hull, to the **Sighting Room**: an eight-sided chamber under the shaft, with a stone basin big enough to sit in, dry, and in the bottom of it, her bath plug. *("My plug.")* She plugs the Basin. Water rises from the shaft's seep. The moon's reflection slides into it, off-center. It's a few nights past full now, so the moon rises later and farther along the horizon; the Keeper's ledger has a line for exactly that ("Past full: allow two fingers"), and Cheryl, who has watched thirty-one moonrises, knows what two fingers looks like. She turns the trim wheel a quarter turn, then another, until the moon sits where it should. The Basin's moonglass rim lights and every lamp Downstairs blazes. Only now does the Keepers' master stop beside the Basin come free: a bronze rod marked with the Middle Sister's emblem. Cheryl pulls it. *Middle sister's greedy, put her back to bed.* Far off, the Middle Sister coughs and goes quiet, and the city settles to its proper waterline. Far above, the canals refill, and the Boatman rises with a piano in his boat. ("I'M AFLOAT! AND I'VE GOT A PIANO!")

Cheryl takes off her shoes and gets into the Basin. *(Internal monologue, the last: a few short lines. She doesn't explain anything. See sample lines.)* She leaves her plug in the Basin, where it now belongs.

Beside the Basin, the Keepers' sighting log ends with *Moon true in the bowl*, signed B. If she likes, she writes under it: *Rang at the turn. Moon true in the bowl. All well.* and a C. (A grace note for Brannoc and the Keepers; optional, never pointed out.)

At dawn she rides the Keepers' counterweighted lift up its side shaft and steps out of the well in Cistern Court. The Old Man looks up from his chess: "Morning."

**Epilogue.** Tessel explains the night to itself, wrongly. The Mayor credits his clock, which has been removed. The Clockmaker is delighted that the sun has "come round." Ambrose, back on the rope, sums up the night in the last Keeper's own words without knowing it: "RANG AT THE TURN. ALL WELL." Ottilie announces the bootleggers. The Shopkeeper makes his first sale in thirty years (a gift): a new bath plug. The Kid wins the Stair Race, counted home step by step by the Counting Woman, now its official counter. Her Next Door's sheets are the whitest in history. Mott's ferry lands on the first try; he looks at the Gate and keeps his mouth shut. The Pelletiers come home. The Boatman delivers the piano by boat; the Piano Movers carry it up to No. 9, and the Old Man says it's from Gerald: "Gerald heard you play once. Through the wall. Years ago." *(Description: "Gerald has very good manners for a man who doesn't exist.")*

**Final image.** Cheryl at her table with the moon chart adds a tick in the Gate (two fingers past, for the date). It isn't Moonshine Night. "But it counts." Then a card: *Moonshine Night. A year later.* The Act 1 view from her roof, made right: Cheryl *in* her tub this time, with a new plug, the moon rising dead in the Gate, and the rhyme's tune on a music box, in tune at last. The ritual bath stolen in the opening is finally taken; the Basin bath stays the unexpected one. The credits end: *No one ever knew.*

No one ever knows.

## Tone and humor

### Guidelines

- **Warm first, funny second, mysterious throughout.** Every joke comes from someone's character. No winking at the player, no pop-culture references, no fourth-wall breaks, no anachronisms.
- **Each NPC has one comic engine** (see the cast table). Return to it; escalate it; let it reveal something human. The Old Man's chess is about loneliness. The Lighthouse Keeper's polishing is about not wanting to look at the sea. Don't explain that; let it be there.
- **Nobody is a villain.** The antagonist is forgetting. The Mayor is vain, not wicked; the Clockmaker is proud, not cruel.
- **Wrong explanations are the main running joke.** Residents always have a confident wrong theory for each symptom. The player knows more than everyone, and that's the pleasure.
- **Downstairs is quiet.** Humor drops to almost nothing underground. Descriptions get shorter and more plain. Awe needs room. The first time Cheryl comes back up, let the noise of the city hit hard.
- **Mystery is played straight.** No character ever says "the city is a ship" until the player has seen the mosaic. Clues are planted early and in plain sight. No exposition dumps; the Keeper's ledger and the mosaic do the telling.
- **Brevity.** One script line must fit in one text box: three wrapped lines of the real font, 224 px wide (roughly 110–120 characters, fewer in capitals). Aim for about 90. Most NPC speeches should be four boxes or fewer.
- **Repeat talks are short** and change when the world changes. Every NPC has something new to say after each act's big event.
- **Age is not a joke** except when Cheryl makes it about herself.

Cheryl, descriptions:

Cheryl, Downstairs:

> *…Ah.*

> *The best bath of my life is in a stone bowl at the bottom of the city, with my own plug.* / *Nobody will ever know.* / *Most of the best things, nobody ever knows.*

NPCs:

> **AMBROSE:** CLOCKS DON'T KNOW THE SEA. THE SEA DOESN'T KNOW CLOCKS. ONE OF THEM IS IN CHARGE AND IT ISN'T THE CLOCK.

## Text conventions

- NPC lines show the speaker's label in capitals (OLD MAN, NEIGHBOR, AMBROSE).
- Cheryl's descriptions have no label.
- Internal monologue is in italics or a distinct soft color, with no label (the engine's choice).
- Ambrose's lines are always written in capitals. Nobody else shouts like that except at peak moments (one word, rarely).
- One "line" in a script equals one text box, and must fit in three wrapped lines (see Brevity).
- Check unusual characters against the font (`game/src/art/font.js`) before using them in game text. As of this writing it lacks "×", "–" (en dash), and "→"; it has "·", "…", "—", and "♪".
- American English spelling.
- **Describe what's drawn.** A look line must agree with the art: avoid counts and colors unless the art shows them plainly ("thirty clocks" over ten drawn, or a "green" bell drawn brown, reads as a bug). (From the QA description sweep, A6UaxuFHMT.)
- **A line that names a state goes stale.** Any line that mentions the tide, the light, the time, or someone's presence or absence ("Mott's sitting on the good one") has to be checked, and changed if needed, in every later act and the epilogue, where the world it describes may have moved on.

## Decisions log

Creative decisions made in this document, with their reasons. The client's brief requirements are marked **(brief)**.

**Client feedback after playing the opening** (relayed by the orchestrator):
- **Cheryl must read as a vigorous late-50s woman,** dry and capable, never frail or elderly. Every line that leaned on her age ("at my age," aching knees and arms, "not at fifty-eight") is rewritten across all acts; see "Vigorous" under Cheryl.
- **The sky must be physically right** apart from where Tessel is. The full moon rises in the east as the sun sets in the west; the vistas and tide times follow "The sky over Tessel."
- **The audio is "pretty good":** keep the [audio direction](audio-direction.md) as it is.

**Client feedback after playing to Act 4** (relayed by the orchestrator; the full notes and the reasoning are in [the structure pass](design/structure-pass.md)):
- **Every character has a job in a puzzle or the plot, and the middle acts run several threads at once.** The Lighthouse Keeper, the Shopkeeper, the Man on Bench, and the Woman with Watering Can get Act 2 jobs; Ottilie points the way in Act 4.
- **Cheryl's own hat is no longer a puzzle answer.** The client found four early puzzles amounted to handing over her belongings. The Tourist's hat now comes from the Shop window by a chain of trades. The soap stays: it's set up in Act 1 and it's the funniest set piece in Act 2.
- **The Back is the west end, and the Goat Path comes down it into the west end of the Bilge.** The Harbor is east of the Bilge. The Goat Path is a natural cliff path, taller than the Hundred Steps.

- **The city is Tessel, on an island in a sheltered bay; it is secretly a moored floating ship built by the residents' own ancestors.** This gives one coherent mystery that explains every symptom, the tunnels' different style **(brief)**, the forgetting, and a surface change that's physically spectacular (the canals drain). The "residents are descendants" twist keeps the history human rather than alien.
- **The Wrongness is a slipped mooring: a broken pawl, failing ballast, and no sighting.** It's concrete enough to puzzle around (parts, valves, timing) and escalates naturally (drift, then list, then rise, then the reef).
- **Cheryl's goal is her yearly Moonshine Night bath; the payoff is the Sighting Basin with her own plug.** It's small, personal, funny, and specific **(brief: small personal stakes; achieved unexpectedly at the end)**. The lost plug gives a comic thread that runs under the whole game.
- **The title "Moonshine" (fixed by the project) is used five ways:** the festival, the rhyme, Mott's spirit, "that's moonshine" meaning nonsense, and the Founders' glyph.
- **"Two jobs" is a design rule:** every Founder object has a forgotten second job, which is the source of puzzle solutions.
- **Rituals are degraded instructions:** the bell, the festival, the rhyme, the Count, and the Stair Race. This is how the residents unknowingly help save the city they don't know is in danger **(brief: residents never know)**.
- **Cheryl is a retired primary teacher who came from the mainland.** She knows everyone since they were seven, which drives her voice, and she's an outsider who notices.
- **Cheryl's past has deliberate gaps** (the second hand on the chart, the second chair, the face-down photo, the song, the piano). These must never be resolved **(brief: let players fill in gaps)**. Keep the numbers apart too: the Old Man's game with Gerald is seventeen years old, so it can't be read as starting when the second handwriting stopped (twelve years ago).
- **The Sighting Shaft rises inside No. 9; the court well is the Keepers' lift shaft and vent.** That way the plug's fall, the roof's view, the breathing well, and the final exit all fit the opening map. (From review by xaKh8TS_PJ.)
- **The full moon's spring tide breaks the Tooth,** so the disaster falls on Moonshine Night because of the moon, not by coincidence. (From review.)
- **The fountain valve is a required step, and it jams,** so Cheryl's complicity in the Fountain stopping is unavoidable. **The Fountain refills at the reveal** because the flooded Pump Hall backs up through its feed. (From review.)
- **Cheryl stops the Middle Sister herself** in Act 6 with the Keepers' master stop, so the rhyme's "put her back to bed" is a player action. (From review.)
- **Three named NPCs (Ambrose, Ottilie, Mott)**; everyone else is descriptive **(brief)**.
- **No deadlines, no death, no fail states.** Time of day changes only at scripted moments. The one timed moment (the Kid's relay) is the Kid's run, not Cheryl's, and can be retried.
- **Period:** vaguely a century ago, with no electricity. This keeps the SNES-era storybook feel and makes a clock a plausible "modernization."
